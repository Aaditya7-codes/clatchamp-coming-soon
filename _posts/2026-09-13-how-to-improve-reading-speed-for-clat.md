Reading speed matters in CLAT because you will read roughly **twenty passages of 450 words in two hours**, and answer 120 questions on them. But speed pursued on its own reliably lowers scores, and most "speed reading" advice does exactly that.

Here is the version that works.

## Measure first

You cannot improve what you have not measured, and almost nobody measures this.

**The test.** Take an unseen 450-word passage with five questions.
1. Time your reading only — stop the clock before the questions.
2. Note the seconds.
3. Answer the questions, then mark them.
4. Record **both** numbers: reading time and accuracy.

Do this weekly, same conditions. Two numbers, one line each week.

## What the numbers should look like

At a comfortable working pace, **450 words in about 2 to 2.5 minutes** leaves a sensible budget: with five questions at roughly 45 seconds each, a passage block lands near six minutes. Twenty blocks fills two hours with a little spare.

That is the target to work toward. But it is the second number that governs everything:

**If reading time falls while accuracy falls, you have not improved. You have traded.** This is the trap. Speed is easy to increase and easy to measure, so it is the number people chase — while the number that decides the mark quietly drops.

Only treat a faster time as progress if accuracy holds or rises.

## What actually increases speed

**Fewer regressions.** Most slow reading is not slow eyes, it is re-reading — the eye jumping back because attention lapsed. Reading in a quiet place with the phone elsewhere does more for speed than any technique.

**Familiarity with the prose style.** A passage on monetary policy is slow the first time you meet that vocabulary and faster the tenth. This is why [reading widely](/blog/how-to-prepare-for-clat-english/) beats drilling: it is the only way to make more subjects familiar.

**Reading in phrases rather than words.** Your eye takes in a group of words per fixation. This widens naturally with practice; it does not need a technique bought from a course.

**One pass, done properly.** Reading once with full attention is faster than reading twice quickly, and comprehension is much better. This is the most under-used speed technique there is.

## What does not work

**Skimming.** CLAT questions turn on inference and tone, which live in the parts skimming skips.

**Subvocalisation-suppression drills.** Popular in speed-reading courses; the evidence that they help comprehension is thin, and they cost attention you need.

**Pointer and pacer methods.** They increase eye speed. They do not increase understanding, and the paper tests understanding.

## The realistic timeline

Reading speed improves slowly — over months, not weeks. A candidate who starts in [Class 11](/blog/how-to-prepare-for-clat-from-class-11/) has time for it to compound. A candidate starting six months out should spend their effort on question technique, where returns arrive faster, and accept the reading speed they have.

## The daily habit that does it

One article a day, read once, properly, followed by the argument in one sentence. Fifteen minutes. It builds comprehension and speed together, which is the only combination worth having.
