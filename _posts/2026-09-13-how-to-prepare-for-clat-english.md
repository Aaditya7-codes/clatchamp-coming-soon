The [English section](/blog/what-is-the-english-section-in-clat/) is comprehension, not grammar, and comprehension is built by reading. That sounds too simple to be a plan, so here is the specific version.

## The one exercise that matters

Read one substantial article a day. Then, before you look at anything else, **write the author's argument in a single sentence**:

> *The author argues X, because Y.*

If you cannot, read it again. That is the entire exercise, and it is worth more than any number of practice questions.

Why it works: every question type in the section — [main idea, inference, tone, purpose](/blog/clat-english-topics/) — depends on having held the argument in your head. Candidates who miss these questions usually did not misjudge the options. They never had a firm grip on what the passage was doing.

Fifteen minutes a day. It is not negotiable and it is not replaceable.

## What to read

**Editorials and opinion columns.** Argumentative, roughly the right length, and structurally close to what the paper uses.

**Long-form essays and serious magazine journalism.** Harder than exam passages, which is the point — train above the target.

**Non-fiction on unfamiliar subjects.** The exam will hand you a passage on architectural history or monetary policy. Comfort with not-knowing-the-topic is itself a skill.

**Not novels, mostly.** Enjoyable and good for you, but narrative prose trains a different muscle than argumentative prose.

## Why vocabulary lists disappoint

Vocabulary appears in CLAT chiefly as **meaning in context**. A word met three times in real sentences is usable; a word memorised once from a list is recognised but not understood — and recognition is exactly what the distractor options exploit.

Keep a running list of words you meet *while reading*, with the sentence you met them in. That works. Learning a thousand words in isolation mostly does not.

## Building speed without losing accuracy

Speed comes from fewer regressions — fewer times your eye jumps back. It does not come from skimming.

The measurable version: time yourself on a 450-word passage. Note the time and how many questions you got right. Track both for a month. If speed rises while accuracy falls, you are not improving; you are trading. There is a fuller method in [how to improve reading speed for CLAT](/blog/how-to-improve-reading-speed-for-clat/).

## A realistic weekly shape

- **Six days:** one article, one sentence summarising it. 15–20 minutes.
- **Three days:** add a timed practice passage with questions. 10 minutes.
- **Once a week:** something genuinely difficult — a long essay on an unfamiliar subject. 30 minutes.

Under three hours a week. Sustainable for two years, which matters more than intensity, because this is the section that cannot be crammed.

## Why this section deserves the most time

English is 22–26 questions directly. But reading ability determines your performance in [Legal Reasoning](/blog/what-is-legal-reasoning-in-clat/), [Logical Reasoning](/blog/what-is-logical-reasoning-in-clat/), [Current Affairs](/blog/what-is-current-affairs-and-gk-in-clat/) and even the data passages in [Quantitative Techniques](/blog/what-are-quantitative-techniques-in-clat/).

Improve at reading and you have improved at all 120 questions. Nothing else on the syllabus does that.
