Current Affairs including General Knowledge is, alongside Legal Reasoning, the **largest section of CLAT** — roughly 28–32 of the 120 questions, about a quarter of the paper.

It is also the section where the most preparation effort is wasted, for one structural reason.

## It is a passage-based section

Questions are not standalone trivia. You are given a passage of roughly 450 words — drawn from news reports, journalistic writing or other non-fiction — and asked a set of questions based on it.

Some of those questions can be answered from the passage alone. Others require you to know something related that the passage does not state. That mix is the whole design: it rewards someone who has followed the news with understanding, not someone who has memorised a list of dates.

## Why most "CLAT current affairs" material is the wrong shape

Search for current affairs preparation and you will find bullet-point compilations: fifty facts from August, a hundred from September. They are easy to produce and easy to revise, and they train the wrong thing.

The exam gives you a passage and asks you to reason around it. A fact list trains recall in isolation, which is a poor rehearsal for reading 450 words under time pressure and answering five questions on them.

The fix is not to stop reading compilations. It is to make sure a real share of your practice is in **passage form**, because that is the format you will meet. More on that in [how to prepare current affairs for CLAT](/blog/how-to-prepare-current-affairs-for-clat/).

## What the passages tend to cover

- National affairs and politics
- International relations and global events
- Legal and constitutional developments
- Economy and business
- Science, technology and environment
- Awards, sport and significant appointments

There is more detail in [CLAT GK topics](/blog/clat-gk-topics/).

## How far back does it go?

Broadly, the twelve months before the paper, with the heaviest weight on the six to eight months immediately preceding it. That makes it the one section where starting early has a limit — current affairs from two years before the exam is mostly no longer current.

## Static GK: how much matters?

Less than most candidates assume, but not zero. Static general knowledge tends to appear where it supports a current story — the office someone has just been appointed to, the treaty a new agreement amends.
