Most candidates lose [Legal Reasoning](/blog/what-is-legal-reasoning-in-clat/) marks for the same reason, and it is not a gap in legal knowledge. It is the opposite: they know something about the law, and they let it override the principle printed in front of them.

The method below is short. The difficulty is not understanding it — it is not abandoning it under time pressure.

## Read the principle twice, before the facts

The instinct is to read the facts first, because they are a story and stories are easier to hold. Resist it. Read the facts first and you form a view about who deserves to win, then bend the principle to reach it.

Read the principle. Read it again. Then, **before going near the facts**, ask:

> What exactly has to be true for this principle to apply?

## Break the principle into conditions

Every principle is a list of conditions in disguise. Take:

> A person who voluntarily accepts a known risk cannot later claim compensation for harm arising from that risk.

That is three conditions:

1. acceptance was **voluntary**
2. the risk was **known**
3. the harm **arose from that risk**

Three conditions, three places the facts can fail. Most questions turn on exactly one of them; the rest of the passage is there to occupy you.

## Then test the facts against each condition

Go through them one at a time and find the words in the fact pattern that satisfy or defeat each. Not a general impression of the situation — the specific words.

This is also how you catch the standard trap: a wrong option that reaches a **sensible outcome by reasoning the principle does not support**. On a question that asks you to apply a principle, the route is the answer.

## When the principle contradicts what you know

Follow the principle. Every time.

This feels wrong, and for candidates who have read widely it feels especially wrong. But the section is not testing whether you know the law. It is testing whether you can take a rule you have just been handed and apply it consistently to a situation you have never seen — which is most of what legal work is, and why the paper asks for it.

## The error log, tagged by trap

This is what turns practice into improvement. For every wrong answer, write one line naming **which trap you fell for**:

- **Added fact** — I relied on something the passage did not state
- **Ignored exception** — the principle carved out an exception and I stepped past it
- **Swapped subject** — I applied the principle to the wrong party
- **Right outcome, wrong route** — the conclusion was fine, the reasoning was not mine to use
- **Outside knowledge** — I used law I knew instead of the principle given
- **Ran out of time** — not a reasoning error, and it needs a different fix

After fifty entries you will find you have two recurring traps, not six. Then you know exactly what to watch for. More on the mechanics in [how to analyse a CLAT mock test](/blog/how-to-analyse-a-clat-mock-test/).

## A short drill

Before choosing an option, write one sentence naming the condition the question turns on. If you cannot name it, you are guessing — however confident the guess feels.

Candidates who do this consistently tend to find accuracy moves before speed does. That is the right order.

## What not to spend time on

Memorising case law. [Legal Reasoning topics for CLAT](/blog/legal-reasoning-topics-for-clat/) explains why lists of landmark judgments are largely the wrong preparation for this section — the passage gives you what you need, and outside knowledge actively misleads.
