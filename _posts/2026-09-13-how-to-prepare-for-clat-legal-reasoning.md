Prepare [Legal Reasoning](/blog/what-is-legal-reasoning-in-clat/) by identifying the passage’s rules, testing facts against their conditions and comparing the options. Prior legal knowledge is not required; general awareness can help, but must not replace the passage’s stated rules.

The method below is short. The difficulty is not understanding it — it is not abandoning it under time pressure.

## Identify the rule and its context

The actual paper uses passages; rules and facts are not always separated into labelled boxes. You may need to infer the rule from the discussion.

Read the relevant passage carefully, returning to a rule when necessary. Ask:

> What exactly has to be true for this principle to apply?

## Break the principle into conditions

For a simplified, hypothetical exercise, take this rule:

> A person who voluntarily accepts a known risk cannot later claim compensation for harm arising from that risk.

That is three conditions:

1. acceptance was **voluntary**
2. the risk was **known**
3. the harm **arose from that risk**

Check all three conditions and any exceptions. A question can depend on more than one detail; do not assume the rest of the passage is irrelevant.

## Then test the facts against each condition

Go through them one at a time and find the words in the fact pattern that satisfy or defeat each. Not a general impression of the situation — the specific words.

This is also how you catch the standard trap: a wrong option that reaches a **sensible outcome by reasoning the principle does not support**. On a question that asks you to apply a principle, the route is the answer.

## When the principle contradicts what you know

Follow the principle. Every time.

This feels wrong, and for candidates who have read widely it feels especially wrong. But the section is not testing whether you know the law. It is testing whether you can take a rule you have just been handed and apply it consistently to a situation you have never seen — as required by this passage-based task.

## The error log, tagged by trap

This is what turns practice into improvement. For every wrong answer, write one line naming **which trap you fell for**:

- **Added fact** — I relied on something the passage did not state
- **Ignored exception** — the principle carved out an exception and I stepped past it
- **Swapped subject** — I applied the principle to the wrong party
- **Right outcome, wrong route** — the conclusion was fine, the reasoning was not mine to use
- **Outside knowledge** — I used law I knew instead of the principle given
- **Ran out of time** — not a reasoning error, and it needs a different fix

Review your entries to see which errors recur; there is no fixed number of entries or traps that applies to everyone. More on the mechanics in [how to analyse a CLAT mock test](/blog/how-to-analyse-a-clat-mock-test/).

## A short drill

Before choosing an option, write one sentence naming the condition the question turns on. If you cannot justify the choice, revisit the evidence or identify what remains uncertain.

Check accuracy and time separately so that faster work does not conceal poorer reasoning.

## What not to spend time on

Memorising case law. [Legal Reasoning topics for CLAT](/blog/legal-reasoning-topics-for-clat/) explains why lists of landmark judgments are largely the wrong preparation for this section — the question must be answered from the supplied or inferred rules. Awareness of contemporary legal and moral issues can still help comprehension.
