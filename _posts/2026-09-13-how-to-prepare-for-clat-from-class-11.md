Starting in Class 11 gives you roughly **two years**, and it is the best position to be in — not because you can cover more material, but because [reading ability](/blog/how-to-prepare-for-clat-english/) takes months to build and cannot be rushed at the end.

The risk of a long runway is complacency. Here is a plan that uses it.

## Year one, first half: reading only

**Months 1–8.** Do not start mock tests. Do not buy a shelf of books.

- One substantial article a day, then the argument in one sentence. 15–20 minutes.
- Something harder once a week — a long essay on an unfamiliar subject.
- Read [the exam pattern](/blog/clat-exam-pattern/) once, so you know the shape of what is coming.

That is genuinely all. Eight months of this builds something no amount of Class 12 cramming can replace, and it costs twenty minutes a day.

## Year one, second half: learn the sections

**Months 9–12.**

- Add **[Legal Reasoning](/blog/how-to-prepare-for-clat-legal-reasoning/)** — the largest section and pure technique. Two or three passages a week.
- Add **[Logical Reasoning](/blog/clat-logical-reasoning-syllabus/)** — argument analysis, not puzzles.
- Start the **error log** now, with your first practice questions.
- **Begin current affairs around month 12.** Earlier is largely wasted: news from two years before the paper will not still be current. See [CLAT GK topics](/blog/clat-gk-topics/) for the window.

## Year two, first half: sectional depth and first mocks

**Months 13–18.**

- All five sections in rotation, weighted by your error log rather than your preferences.
- **Monthly full mocks.** Analysis matters more than the score — budget more time for review than for the mock itself.
- Current affairs daily now, and practise it [in passage form](/blog/how-to-prepare-current-affairs-for-clat/).
- Add [Quantitative Techniques](/blog/clat-maths-topics/). A month of regular arithmetic is enough for most.

## Year two, second half: mocks and revision

**Months 19–24.**

- Fortnightly mocks to month 22, then weekly.
- Revision only in the last six weeks. **No new material.**
- Protect your sleep in the final fortnight. It affects reading comprehension more than most candidates believe.

## The part most guides dodge: your boards

Class 11 and 12 are not free. Boards matter for [CLAT eligibility](/blog/clat-eligibility-criteria/) — you need 45% (40% for SC/ST) — and they matter for every alternative path you might take.

Three honest rules:

**During board exam months, CLAT drops to the daily minimum.** Fifteen minutes of reading. Nothing else. Do not attempt to run both at full intensity; you will do both badly.

**CLAT preparation is not a reason to neglect boards.** The downside is asymmetric — a poor board result closes doors that a slightly lower CLAT score does not.

**Use the school holidays.** The long break between Class 11 and 12 is the single most valuable block of time you will get. That is when to do the heavy sectional work.

## The advantage you actually have

Not more content. More **repetitions of reading**. That is the compounding asset, and it is the reason a Class 11 starter with modest daily habits usually outperforms a Class 12 starter with intense ones.
