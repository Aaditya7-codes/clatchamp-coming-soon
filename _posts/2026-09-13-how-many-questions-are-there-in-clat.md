**CLAT has 120 questions, carrying 120 marks, to be answered in 120 minutes.** All multiple-choice, all compulsory, spread across five sections.

## The split

| Section | Questions | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Current Affairs, including General Knowledge | 28–32 | ~25% |
| Legal Reasoning | 28–32 | ~25% |
| English Language | 22–26 | ~20% |
| Logical Reasoning | 22–26 | ~20% |
| Quantitative Techniques | 10–14 | ~10% |

The Consortium publishes weightage rather than a fixed count, so the exact numbers move a little from year to year. Plan against the ranges, not a single figure.

## What that leaves you per question

One minute. That is the headline number, and taken literally it is misleading.

Questions come in sets attached to passages. A typical Legal Reasoning passage carries four to six questions. So the realistic unit is not a question but a **block**: roughly 450 words to read plus five questions to answer, in about six minutes.

Budgeted that way, a two-hour paper is about **20 to 24 blocks**. That is a more useful thing to hold in your head than "one minute per question", because it tells you what to do when a passage is hard: the cost of re-reading is measured against the block, not the question.

## The distribution matters for preparation

Legal Reasoning and Current Affairs together are **half the paper**. Quantitative Techniques is a tenth. If you are allocating study hours and treating all five sections as equal, you are mis-weighting by a factor of three at the extremes.

That does not mean abandoning Quantitative Techniques — ten to fourteen marks decides admissions at the margin. It means being clear-eyed about where an extra hour returns most.
